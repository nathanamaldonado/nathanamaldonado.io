Company Hire

At one point, I thought I wanted to work for a bank. Here are my thoughts on recruitting and men in finance.

May 2025
FILED UNDER
  • Essays

On the drive to Los Angeles International, my Tia Yolanda retold the same story of a boyfriend who had kidnapped her in North Carolina during her twenties. Before leaving me at my terminal, I had to remind her that I was not going to see her old flame, and Durham was nowhere near this mystery town where she had been held. No, I was flying to New York City. Well, Newark.

However, prior to my arrival, the only introduction I had to New York City's culture was through its Bridge and Tunnel youth: Jay and Giovanni. Jay had been born in Jersey City and wore his METROcard on the back of his clear phone case, should you ever forget. However, he was still part of the wealthy multinational scene on campus, having attended boarding school in Lagos, Nigeria and would be living in Bushwick after graduation, working as a technical analyst for J.P. Morgan.

Jay had been my orientation leader, but our Friendship only began this past Valentine's Day when we met at a mutual friend's party. With the whole prep school boy thing going for him, he helped to get a girl friend of mine home, and that night soon began our Friendship. Graduation was soon approaching, and he would often recount stories from New York City as a teenager with access to quality fake IDs and an uninhibited personality.

Eventually, we parted ways, and our goodbye was just that—good and absolute. You would witness my life from beginning to end if you could only hear our last moments together.

Giovanni, however, was born and raised in Westchester and attended Duke through a debate scholarship as a first-generation, low-income college student to conservative Trinidadian and Argentine parents.

They would not be attending his graduation.

Instead, his background and lust for substance complemented his brilliance because that is simply who he was: a brilliant political science major who found that he hated being hungry more than he hated the institution. In fact, he and Jay had been close friends since their first year of college and would also be living in New York City after graduation. However, Giovanni would be living in the East Village as an investment banking analyst (also) working for J.P. Morgan.

The Company, or rather, men at The Company, follow me, not as a ghost but as a crime.

Well, Giovanni chose the East Village, not unlike those who came before him, well documented by Nelson Sullivan throughout the eighties. However, liberation means something different in this age of commercialized identities. So, while Giovanni fights for financial independence, he relinquishes this to J.P. Morgan, leaving me to consider the role and impact of those from the past and how newcomers have shifted these neighborhoods.

In many ways, we have served these institutions and offered our stories while attempting to hold onto our art. Now, Giovanni will be living in his once affordable apartment in a neighborhood where RuPaul roamed while begging for quarters to buy just a slice of pizza.

My Friendship with Giovanni, however, persisted with the same turbulence as my flight from Los Angeles. See, Giovanni was someone or something of a psychopath where only he could make sense of you.

These men are what New York City meant to me.

Upon my arrival, however, I followed the advice of Fatouma, a friend of mine who I met while studying abroad in London. Fatouma attended boarding school in Connecticut on scholarship and, now, Columbia as a native Queensite. Well, I listened to Fatouma in her saying the real Chinatown is in Flushing, Queens.

I took up reading Patti Smith's Just Kids on my journey and observed as people entered and left the train car while reading and rereading her explanation that existing as a muse from time to time guaranteed her legacy in this world: a most terrifying life.

As humans, we often neglect our two most meaningful gifts: language and memory. We strive to control both, so I remained stunned that in this city of brilliance, genius, and art, that any person—Patti Smith or anyone in this train car—would ever choose to relinquish their legacy. How could one ever be satisfied in letting and knowing their memory exists as an extension of someone else's success? How, in this city where even the skyline lives to mark permanence and legacy, could anyone be satisfied in existing in only one person's memory?

Nevertheless, upon looking up from my reading, I noticed 1) "This is the last stop" and 2) I was the only non-Asian person on this 7 Express Train. Well, I trudged up the stairs with a mission and immediately composed myself. I walked up to an older man with a fruit cart squatting and politely asked, "Chinese cigarette?" to which he handed me a box of Newports, which I would have gladly accepted had I hated myself only a little bit more.

No, I persisted. Instead, he clutched a red bag from underneath the mangos and pineapple and made his first offer: $10. After some back and forth with his passionate wife, I pocketed my prize and made my way back to Brooklyn Heights when a businessman in a full suit and tie approached me on the subway.

Once, in Los Angeles, I witnessed a man lose grip of his bag and out fell some fifty-odd knives that scattered the train car like marbles on a tile floor. So, typically, I will have my guard up when riding public transportation as a twenty-one-year-old who has failed the written driver's test thrice. However, I felt distinctly enthused after locating my True Dragons and chose to entertain this conversation.

His personality seemed to ricochet, and we spoke all the way from the Flushing Main Street station to his transfer at Grand Central / 42nd Street. Seemingly, everyone had an opinion about our meeting as we loudly discussed our lives and plans for the future. Though, as a linguist and devout follower of our Lord (Dwight Bolinger), I chose to believe that "truth is a linguistic principle" and became increasingly creative in my responses. Of course, I would not entertain a stranger, especially not one with several bottles of Soju in his bag. No.

However, prior to his exit from the train car, I asked about his line of work, to which I found a most beautiful response:

"Oh, I’m at J.P. Morgan.”
