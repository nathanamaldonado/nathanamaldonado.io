Canopies

Riley convinced me to enroll in a writing class called Spiritual Autobiography. I thought it was going to be Mickey Mouse, but I actually enjoyed talking about the men in my life as I moved about my life's gardens. A whole two and a half hour block to talking about myself.

April 2026

  • Essays

Lined with palms, each garden rolls into the next. Every manicured lawn now flirts with its neighbor, with no fencing to object to their marriage. These palms, my grandfather explained, kept the time. Their heights reveal the duration of our stay, setting the pace for our movement and rooted in our arrival.

My family reached Los Angeles in the eighties after a string of affairs that exiled my grandfather from Miami, meaning I would inherit both our home in Boyle Heights and its hidden room.

Our nursery surrounds the property and stains the pavement with fallen fruit, fallen fruit which often sticks to my soles and stains our hardwood floors. Every summer, my grandmother would remind me to remove my shoes before entering the house. Every weekend, my grandfather advised me the same.

“Take your shoes off if you don’t want your mother to know you’ve left.”

Despite hardly speaking to one another at mealtime, he began leaving our gate unlocked, anticipating debauchery, which he would hear nothing about.

While gardens protect life, the home conceals it.

To avoid a loud click when the hinges latched, I learned to grip the doorknob to the left before closing the gate he had left unlocked.

Eventually, I would retire from the garden and meet a boyfriend or boyfriend’s boyfriend down the street, protected by the palms overhead.

Such was my inheritance.

I never contested my grandfather’s whereabouts, so when he left for Mexico the day before my graduation party, I did not question his disappearance.

Truthfully, the celebrations held in our front yard were an effort to receive pocket money from my relatives for cigarettes and tattoos.

He taught me to keep busy, and I wondered where I would find this freedom once in North Carolina.

The Sarah P. Duke Gardens were formed following the edifice of Duke University’s West Campus in the thirties.

At the site of leftover construction materials, professors at the medical school expressed concern about the adornments of the young campus, declaring that a landfill detracts from a promised promenade.

Today, their legacies nourish generations of stewards who protect the Duke Gardens, with significant donations from professors and alumni in the natural sciences.

These plaques, adorning all benches and bowers, pay respect to that which no longer exists, and which now utter back at us, inviting us to participate in a community of custodians.

Placed far within the Pine Clouds Mountain Stream, an unmarked bench sits between bamboo trees and overlooks the Meyer Bridge.

Those standing on the red overpass rest their forearms on its railings, enhancing its color to its natural cedar and reminding onlookers that many more will follow.

Hardly do they shift their focus from the waterfront and fowl, oblivious of my gaze on a bench still nameless.

We must each feel responsible for the maintenance of these conservatories, a sentiment shared by Chibuike Okafor, a climate scholar and undergraduate representative at the Sarah P. Duke Gardens.

Now a senior, Chibuike affirms that we must watch as these evergreens mature and supervise our stay.

Chibuike and I first met in her home at the Nicholas School of the Environment late into our junior year.

The nights of indecency after fleeing my family’s garden in Southern California persisted into nights of student misconduct on the rooftop of Grainger Hall.

Because of the chapel view, the rooftop garden has become increasingly popular among students prepared to face scolding from climate scientists, environmentalists, and people who do very little.

Our friendship began over several debates on where to crush cigarettes and dispose of the butts.

Could we not simply toss the paper and tobacco into the soil? Over the ledge?

Well, I would keep encountering Chibuike at every social corner and began to admire her fervor as we overwhelmed West Campus together.

In early February, Chibuike and I trod along the frozen walkways of the Duke Gardens, our feet landing inside the snowy footprints left by those before us.

We disregarded our sleeves and wiped the ice as it thawed, finally resting under a gazebo, now slightly wet and most definitely cold.

“How often are you here?” I asked.

“Not too often.”

“What’s not too often?”

“Daily.”

Beyond recognizing that I was vastly underappreciating the time I had in these playgrounds before our graduation, I became curious about who else she must have sat under this bird-watching shelter.

I thought back to the first kiss I shared with a boyfriend under the Blomquist Pavilion and the breakup I had with another.

These routine conversations are my inheritance as the men in my family taught me to love as much as I can in this lifetime.

I thought back to junior high, when we learned about the tiers of a romantic relationship from our outdated textbook, peaking at “going steady.”

I thought back to the boyfriends I hid in my bedroom after thrashing about my grandmother’s hibiscus flowers.

Well, there ought to be an amendment in my health textbook for our style of love, love bombing and all.

Eventually, North Carolina’s tensions eased, and the icicles now softened at every corner of the gazebo’s ceiling.

In abandoning a destination, we accepted stillness.

We positioned ourselves under pavilions, beside flowerbeds, and atop ponds to oversee the evergreens rise.

Our uncertainty about a destination steers our roaming.

We are no longer witnesses to the garden but accepted by her, loved for our abandonment of trivial pursuits.

In our neighborhood, the street lights would turn on, all at once, once evening struck.

I was always home before then; however, I dared not object to her one rule.

At least not until I was a teenager and had become a more skilled actor.

Just as a child without a watch, Chibuike and I relied on the size of our shadows to reveal the time, just as our streetlights once had.

These are the tools we must appreciate, the speech between ourselves and our home.

Chibiuke became a stewardess for the gardens during our junior year, but her love story began long ago.

She raves about the hosted wellness events, which support the Durham community and protect life's moments at every corner, at every moment.

Through partnerships with the oncology and cancer centers at Duke University Hospital, patients are offered an opportunity to recall their happenings, their breakups and weddings, their showers and receptions.

Positioned between Duke University’s West Campus and Hospital, each entrance routinely welcomes memory and embraces storytelling.

By touring patients, families, and classrooms, scholars like Chibuike inspire a younger generation of healers.

She directs us to confront our displacement.

She directs us to consider, “what’s not too often?”

During the quiet period after participating in a full service of worship for these grasslands, we shared our understanding of burial and return.

What is to come of us after we leave the Gardens? After we graduate? And after that?

We finally arrived at the Japanese bridge and fulfilled our script.

Monitoring the waterfront and fowl, we remained oblivious to the unnamed bench facing our backs, peeking through the miniature bamboo forest.

Chibuike begins with religion, then politics, before finally resting on the belief that “we are all organic.”

We must return to what once was, she confesses.

Yet my boyish desire to reach these treetops interferes with my story’s end.

I contend that a burial fails to honor a full life, as Chibuike suggests, and instead offers her an ultimate celebration, finally reaching the towering palms in my family garden.

“I hope one day to be cremated into a firework for everyone to see, for everyone to gather in our garden and witness my goodbye.”

My desire to retire in such a display amongst the palms and spread across these rolling gardens serves as my departure, my withdrawal when I can no longer appreciate fallen fruit, ravaged flowerbeds, or whining gates.

Once in season, my grandfather would order my brothers and me to mount our fig tree and fill my grandmother’s recycled Easter baskets without mangling those at the bottom.

We left those already harvested by bees alone and passed them down an assembly line to our water hose, where my cousins washed away the dirt.

He routinely pruned the tree, which stood twenty feet tall, to plan for a gazebo he would never complete.

My grandmother justified this loss after my cousin fell while playing yard games as a child; he claimed to have seen a ghost peering from our attic window and described the previous owner of our home.

Boyle Heights sits locked in by freeways on either side, absorbing traffic from Downtown Los Angeles, and is recognized as the world’s busiest and most congested interchange.

Historically, immigrant communities have arrived in Boyle Heights to build wealth, including Japanese, Jewish, and Mexican immigrants, but each failed to defend their property rights, and the construction of these interstate systems began in the sixties.

Our home was built by a Japanese doctor and immigrant, which meant the later conversion of the lobby into a foyer and the doctor’s office into a bedroom on the first floor.

So, when my cousin rushed to the attic and began his tale of a ghost sighting—an Asian businessman hiding in our attic, my grandfather locked us out and kept us children from searching for ghosts.

Though, as a teenager, he more than welcomed my search for trouble.

Nearly a decade later, at my graduation party and once all supervision had abandoned my celebration, my brothers and I sat around a campfire in our yard and shared the same few stories as if hearing them for the first time.

We reminded each other about flickering lights, omitting that traffic accidents nearby often caused power outages in our neighborhood.

We laughed and laughed until finally asking, “Would we haunt this house, too?”

We would.

However, these are the sentiments championed by the spiritual, by the self-confessed.

While some inherit these grounds as gatekeepers, like Chibiuke, my brothers, and I, others are trained to deny.

Katherine Henderson, Education Program Coordinator at Duke Gardens and a refusenik of the spiritual and religious, demands the protection of our surroundings, forsaking the idea of protective or haunting spirits.

That which was natural, was.

Our investigation into this natural inheritance, therefore, must never be consolidated, for conflict serves as the social procedure of religion.

Instead, we must not respond to our curiosity about where the world vanishes once our eyes are no longer open.

We can only assume others still appreciate its tall grasses once ours close.

Chibuike introduced me to Katherine, her supervisor, feeling I might be interested in meeting someone with a familiarity in symbolic systems and the scientific exchange between life.

I would soon discover that Katherine herself graduates from a guild of scientists who honor life itself and are unencumbered by the idea of life after death.

Instead, she interests herself in true natural engagements, not the spiritual.

She is a scientist, and faith makes no room for inquiry or contest.

Instead, Katherine relies on what is seen, what is written, and what is proven.

I returned once again to the gardens, now to meet Katherine.

After marching down the stone steps behind the Allen Building to reach Flowers Drive, I caught myself on the railing before crashing down and pretending my jump was intentional.

Katherine had been seated behind the Memorial Gate, surveying pedestrians who had now abandoned her periphery.

Finally, she stood and gestured in my direction, and we began.

Immediately, we stared back at each other, the gardens a forum for decision and pause.

Silently, we negotiated a path towards the Burpee Learning Center, pinned at the opposite corner from where we met.

Now melted away, the absence of hardened snow and chalky ice revealed tricky mud paths and false trails.

Our heels no longer pressed into a crunching frost, but rolled pebbles between the tread patterns of our shoes.

Katherine, a former neuroimaging researcher, now serves as the Education Program Coordinator and previously as a gallery guide at the Nasher Museum of Art.

I became curious about her career path, but became even more hesitant about appearing as a Duke student, hoping to find any opportunity to network.

Still, her passions reached far beyond what can be written on a resume, and she shared them for our environment and service through stewardship.

She began with queer ecology.

I used to believe that everyone was born straight and that when I was younger, I must have missed a secret meeting with all the schoolboys deciding that we found women attractive.

Now, I believe that I am gay to put other men at ease.

I am an actor and a flirt, so people often label me charming or charismatic.

Though my ex-boyfriend and his friends called this infidelity.

Once, tucked away behind the Japanese bridge, I sat with my ex-boyfriend and shared with him what was on my mind: “You know I am seeing other people, right?”

He asked if I thought of him at all, and I knew I was No Good, as Amy Winehouse puts it, when my immediate thought was: I know the answer I am supposed to give, but I am not a liar either.

In my attempts to distance myself from accountability, however, I call upon my brother, another queer man taught about love through the same men throughout our childhood.

Oftentimes, we laugh over the phone, citing that because we will never be parents, we will not be able to pass these lessons to our own.

Instead, we look to our youngest brother, a straight man.

Such is the foundation of queer ecology: a generational exchange, regardless of reproductive behaviors.

Our learning and lessons persist, our memory preserved.

We rely on our youngest brother and fight, then, over who will serve as the fun gay uncle and which will be the chaotic one.

I fear, I know which role I will inherit, and I sincerely apologize to my youngest brother for the corruption of his children, who will know how to roll cigarettes, find a lighter in a crowd, and evade fares.

Queer ecology restructures the family to reflect the interaction humans have with nature, to reflect the interaction nature has with itself.

Queerness, here, does not suggest the violation of a biological standard but promotes love and safety for those around us.

Katherine, therefore, oversees creation.

She oversees the maintenance of community efforts, each connected to the greater cause of a secure future.

She oversees schoolchildren, undergraduate interns, and local community organizations, connecting their service missions to community building and storytelling.

Our conversations paused as a hawk roared above, again and again.

In a few short moments, a squirrel raced ahead of us but unexpectedly wavered, caught between left and right.

A binary judgment led to his capture, yet plenty more led him there.

The hawk soared with the squirrel encased by his claws.

“I have actually never seen that before,” she revealed. “Oh, well, that’s a first.”

Stunned, Katherine clarified her philosophy, explaining that these attitudes about how people ought to think about the world are her own.

Chibuike and I have only just begun our stories of creation, she reminds me.

We part, and my move toward the Memorial Gate a mirror of my arrival.
